Independent or Captive? Choosing Between Insurance Agents Near Me

Quick Answer

A captive agent sells policies from one insurance company, while an independent agent can quote several companies and move you between them. Independent agents usually suit people with complex needs, higher-risk profiles, or homes in tough insurance markets. Captive agents suit people who already like a particular brand and want deep knowledge of that company’s products. Both are licensed and both earn commissions.

Type “insurance agent” into a map app and you will see a mix of offices. Some carry the logo of a big national brand on the door. Others have their own agency name and a row of carrier logos in the window. That difference is not just branding. It shapes how many prices you see, how your agent behaves at renewal, and what happens if your insurer decides it no longer wants your business.

This article breaks down how each model works, where each one shines, and how to decide which kind of agent to call first.

How the Two Models Work

Captive agents

A captive agent has a contract with a single insurance company. The agent may run their own office and employ their own staff, but they can only sell that company’s policies. In return, the company often provides marketing, training, software, and sometimes a steady flow of leads.

Because they work with one carrier every day, captive agents tend to know that company’s coverage options, discounts, and underwriting rules inside out. If you want a specific brand, a captive agent is often the fastest way to get it.

Independent agents

An independent agent holds appointments with multiple insurance companies. Depending on the agency, that might be four carriers or forty. When you ask for a quote, they enter your details into several systems and compare the results.

Independent agencies are businesses in their own right. They own their book of clients, which means if one carrier raises rates sharply or stops writing in your area, the agent can try to move you to another company rather than lose you as a customer.

How big is each channel?

The independent channel is larger than many people realize. The Big “I” 2026 Market Share Report found that independent agencies wrote about 62% of all U.S. property and casualty premiums in 2025. In commercial lines the figure was 87.7%. In personal lines, which covers home and auto for households, it was 39.5%, up from 36.7% in 2021. Within personal lines, independent agents wrote roughly half of homeowners premiums and about a third of personal auto.

Those numbers show two things. First, both channels serve millions of households. Second, independent agents have been gaining ground with homeowners in particular, which lines up with the tougher property markets in states like Texas, Florida, Louisiana, and California.

Side-by-Side: What Changes for You

Choice and price

This is the most obvious difference. A captive agent can show you one company’s price, possibly with a few package options. An independent agent can show you several.

More quotes do not always mean a lower price. A big captive carrier may simply be the cheapest option for your profile. But when your situation is unusual, comparing several companies often uncovers a better mix of price and coverage than any single insurer offers.

Depth of product knowledge

Captive agents usually win here. They see the same forms, endorsements, and discount rules every day. If a carrier offers a niche add-on, such as coverage for a home-sharing rental or a particular type of rideshare gap, the captive agent will know exactly how it works.

Independent agents know a wider range of products, but may not know every endorsement from every company as closely. Good independent agencies solve this by having staff who specialize in particular carriers or lines.

Service at renewal

Renewal is where the models really split. If your premium jumps 25%, a captive agent can review your discounts, adjust your deductible, or tweak coverage, but cannot move you to a different company.

An independent agent can do all of that and also re-shop your policy across their carriers. In markets where rates have been climbing fast, that ability can save real money year after year.

Help with claims

Both types of agents can help with a claim, but their position is slightly different. A captive agent works within one company’s system and often has strong internal contacts. An independent agent works for you as the client and may feel freer to push back on an adjuster, because their relationship with you does not depend on any one insurer.

In practice, the individual agent’s habits matter more than the model. Ask any agent directly how involved they get when a customer files a claim.

When the insurer pulls out

Non-renewals have become common in several states as carriers reduce their exposure to hurricanes, hail, and wildfire. If your captive carrier drops you, your captive agent cannot help you find a replacement. You have to start over.

An independent agent can usually place you with another carrier, or with the state’s insurer of last resort if needed, without you starting from scratch. For homeowners in high-risk areas, this alone can be a strong reason to work with an independent agency.

Who Is a Better Fit for a Captive Agent

Captive agents tend to be a strong fit if:

  • You already know and trust a particular insurance brand.
  • Your situation is straightforward: a standard home, a couple of vehicles, a clean driving record.
  • You want to bundle several products, such as home, auto, life, and an umbrella, under one company for a multi-policy discount.
  • You value the backing of a large national company with extensive digital tools.

Who Is a Better Fit for an Independent Agent

Independent agents tend to be a strong fit if:

  • You live in an area where insurers are raising rates or not renewing policies.
  • Your home is older, has an older roof, or sits near the coast or in a wildfire zone.
  • You have a teen driver, a past accident, a lapse in coverage, or a violation on your record.
  • You own a small business, rental property, boat, RV, or other assets that may need specialty coverage.
  • You want someone to re-shop your policy every year or two without you having to ask.

What Both Types Have in Common

It is easy to frame this as a battle, but the two models have more in common than not. Both:

  • Must hold a state license and complete continuing education.
  • Are paid mostly by commission from the insurance company.
  • Can give you advice on coverage limits, deductibles, and discounts.
  • Can be excellent or poor depending on the individual.

The best captive agent in your town will serve you better than a careless independent agent, and the reverse is just as true. The model sets the range of options. The person sets the quality of service.

How to Decide in Practice

Here is a simple way to choose:

  1. Start with your situation. If it is simple, a captive agent is a perfectly good place to start. If it is complex or your area is a hard market, start with an independent agent.
  2. Get at least two perspectives. Speaking to one captive and one independent agent gives you a quick sense of the market and a benchmark for price and coverage.
  3. Compare like for like. Make sure both quotes use the same dwelling amount, liability limits, and deductibles. Different numbers make the cheaper quote look better than it really is.
  4. Judge the conversation. The agent who asks the most thoughtful questions about your life is usually the one who will protect you best.

A Note on Switching

Moving from a captive agent to an independent one, or the other way round, is usually simple. Your new agent writes a new policy with a start date that matches the end of your old one, and you cancel the old policy in writing. Avoid any gap in coverage, even a single day, because insurers look at continuous coverage when setting your price. If you have a mortgage, make sure your lender gets proof of the new policy so it does not buy expensive force-placed coverage on your behalf.

Common Myths About Agent Types

A few beliefs come up again and again when people compare agents. Most of them do not hold up.

“Independent agents always find the cheapest price.” They see more prices, but if the cheapest carrier for your profile is a large direct or captive brand they do not represent, they will not be able to quote it. More options improve your odds. They do not guarantee the lowest number.

“Captive agents are just salespeople for one company.” Many captive agents run their own small businesses, live in the communities they serve, and build relationships with families over decades. Their advice on coverage can be every bit as careful as an independent agent’s.

“Big brands pay claims better.” Claims service varies by company, and some smaller regional carriers score very well on customer satisfaction. Size alone tells you little. Complaint data and reviews tell you more.

“Switching agents means losing my history.” Your claims and driving history follow you through industry databases regardless of which agent you use. Switching agents does not wipe the slate clean, but it does not erase loyalty you built with the insurer either, as long as you stay with the same company.

“You have to pick one and stay forever.” You can work with a captive agent for auto and an independent agent for a hard-to-place home, or move between them as your needs change. What matters is that each policy is well placed and nothing falls through the gaps.

The Bottom Line

Captive agents offer depth with one company. Independent agents offer breadth across many. Neither is right for everyone. Think about how complex your needs are, how stable the insurance market is where you live, and how much you value having options at renewal. Then pick the agent, not just the model, who listens best.

FAQs

What is the difference between an independent and a captive insurance agent?

A captive agent sells policies from one insurance company. An independent agent has contracts with several companies and can compare quotes across them.

Are independent insurance agents more expensive?

Not usually. Insurers set the premium, and agents of both types are paid mainly by commission from the carrier. Comparing several carriers can often lower your price.

Can a captive agent shop other insurance companies for me?

No. A captive agent can only sell policies from the company they represent, although some can refer you elsewhere for products their company does not offer.

Is State Farm or Allstate an independent agent?

Agents who sell under one big brand’s name are typically captive agents of that company. Check the agent’s website or ask them directly how many carriers they represent.

Should I use an independent agent if my home insurance was non-renewed?

It is often a smart move. An independent agent can quote multiple carriers that may still be writing policies in your area.
